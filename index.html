Makeup, Then & Now
A history in contrasts

The same goal, a century apart from itself

Makeup has always done the same job: help people present themselves the way they want to be seen. What has changed, again and again, is the chemistry, the tools, and the idea of who gets to be beautiful. This page walks through that change, one honest comparison at a time — no products, no brands, just how things used to be done and how they're done today.

What it's made of

Ingredients and safety

The biggest difference between old and new makeup isn't the color — it's what's actually inside the container, and how much anyone checked before it reached a shelf.

Then
  • Formulas commonly contained lead, mercury, or arsenic-based compounds, often to whiten skin or preserve color.
  • Little to no regulatory testing existed before a product went on sale.
  • Ingredient lists were rarely printed or explained to the buyer.
  • Reactions and long-term harm were discovered by users, not labs.
Now
  • Formulas go through dermatological and safety testing before release.
  • Full ingredient lists are printed on packaging and searchable online.
  • Skincare ingredients — hyaluronic acid, SPF, peptides — are blended directly into makeup.
  • Independent watchdogs and regulators can flag unsafe products publicly.
Who it was made for

Shade ranges and who got left out

For most of makeup's history, "your shade" meant whichever of three or four options was closest. That has changed more in the last decade than in the previous fifty years.

Then
  • Foundation and powder ranges typically offered a handful of shades.
  • Products were designed around a single, narrow beauty standard.
  • Darker and deeper skin tones were routinely underserved or ignored.
Now
  • Extended shade ranges — often 20 to 50+ options — are now common at launch.
  • Undertone matching (warm, cool, neutral) is standard shopping language.
  • Inclusivity is treated as a baseline expectation, not a special edition.
How it was applied

Tools and technique

The tools in a makeup bag say a lot about how much control and precision people were expected to want.

Then
  • Fingertips, small pads, and stiff brushes did most of the work.
  • Blending was minimal — colors were applied more flatly, in blocks.
  • A hand mirror or vanity mirror was the only feedback tool available.
  • Techniques were passed down informally, through family or magazines.
Now
  • Sponges, dense synthetic brushes, and airbrush tools allow fine blending.
  • Layering techniques (contour, color-correct, set, bake) are widely taught.
  • Ring lights and front-facing cameras act as a second, brutally honest mirror.
  • Technique is learned from step-by-step video tutorials, at any skill level.
What it was supposed to achieve

The idea of "a good look"

Makeup used to sell one look at a time. Today it sells the idea that a look is something you choose, not something you're handed.

Then
  • Film stars and studio publicity largely set the one "correct" look of an era.
  • Bold red lips or a defined brow were treated as universal finishing touches.
  • Deviating from the current look could be read as careless or unfashionable.
Now
  • "No-makeup makeup," bold color, and editorial styles all coexist as valid choices.
  • Influencers and everyday creators shape trends alongside celebrities.
  • Makeup is talked about as self-expression rather than a single obligation.
How it reached people

Shopping, testing, and technology

Buying makeup used to mean trusting a counter clerk's judgment. Now it can mean previewing a color on your own face before you spend a rupee.

Then
  • Department store counters were often the only place to see and test products.
  • Staff recommendations carried enormous weight over what people bought.
  • Returns and refunds were rare, so a wrong shade was simply a loss.
  • Product information traveled through print ads and word of mouth.
Now
  • Online shade-matching tools and augmented-reality try-on reduce guesswork.
  • Independent reviews and swatch videos are available before purchase.
  • Direct-to-door delivery and easier returns lower the risk of trying something new.
  • Ingredient scanners and apps let anyone check a formula in seconds.
What happens after

Packaging and afterthought

Even the question of what happens once a product is empty is a fairly recent one to take seriously.

Then
  • Compacts and cases were sometimes reused, but mostly out of necessity, not principle.
  • Packaging waste and animal testing were not part of the public conversation.
  • Products rarely disclosed how or where they were manufactured.
Now
  • Refillable compacts and recyclable packaging are actively marketed features.
  • Cruelty-free and vegan formulation claims are commonly disclosed upfront.
  • Supply chains and sourcing are increasingly part of what buyers ask about.
In brief

A short timeline of change

Early 1900s

Makeup becomes mass-produced

Cosmetics move out of home recipes and into factory-made tins, tubes, and compacts sold across department stores.

1940s – 1950s

One look, widely copied

Studio-era film glamour defines a fairly uniform standard: red lips, arched brows, powdered skin — with little shade variety.

1970s – 1980s

Regulation starts to catch up

Growing awareness of harmful ingredients leads to the first serious safety rules and ingredient disclosure requirements in several countries.

2000s

The internet opens the counter

Reviews, forums, and early tutorial videos let ordinary people compare notes that used to live only with sales staff.

2010s – today

Inclusive ranges and digital try-on

Extended shade ranges, augmented-reality previews, and ingredient-conscious formulas become expected, not exceptional.

Same purpose, better-informed choices

Old makeup wasn't foolish, and new makeup isn't automatically perfect — every era does its best with what it knows. What's genuinely different today is the amount of information, safety testing, and choice available to the person holding the product. Knowing that history is part of using it well.
